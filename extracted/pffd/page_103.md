When debt is used for investing in your future, I call it good debt (see Chapter 2).
Borrowing money to pay for an education, to buy real estate, or to invest in
a small business is like eating fruits and vegetables for their vitamins. That’s
not to say that you can’t get yourself into trouble when using good debt.
Just as you can gorge yourself on too much “good food,” you can absolutely
develop financial indigestion from too much good debt.
In this chapter, I mainly help you battle the pervasive problem of consumer
debt. Getting rid of your bad debts may be even more difficult than giving up
the junk foods you love. But in the long run, you’ll be glad you did; you’ll be
financially healthier and emotionally happier. And after you get rid of your
high-cost consumer debts, make sure you practice the best way to avoid
future credit problems: Don’t borrow with bad debt.
Before you decide which debt reduction strategies make sense for you, you
must first consider your overall financial situation (see Chapter 2) and assess
your alternatives. (I discuss strategies for reducing your current spending —
which help you free up more cash to pay down your debts — in the next
chapter.)
